## Judgment calls

1. **Pattern: "The problem moves."** The web removed the publisher's gate on purpose; the work of checking truth and trust moved to every reader. Alternatives considered: "Cheap now, paid later" (free content paid for with data) fits the ad business, but that business model was not Berners-Lee's decision, so pinning it on him would judge the wrong decision.
2. **Foresight is "unforeseeable," per the record, but a reviewer could argue "partly."** Email spam (1978) and computer fraud predated the web, so openness inviting abuse was not unimaginable. The card is careful to say "not at this scale" and cites the 1993 server count and 1994 first banner ad rather than claiming nobody could have imagined misuse.
3. **The $20.9 billion figure is internet crime, not web-only crime.** IC3 counts all internet-enabled crime (email, apps, crypto), and complaints are mostly but not only from the US. Card wording keeps the exact scope: "reported to the FBI's internet crime center." A stricter reviewer might want a web-specific cost number; none as clean was found.
4. **ITU's 6 billion is people using the internet**, not the web specifically. Labeled "online," not "on the web."
5. **Netcraft "sites" are hostnames that answered its survey,** many parked or inactive. Labeled "sites counted by Netcraft," not "websites in use."
6. **The public domain release was CERN's decision** (signed by two CERN directors), not Berners-Lee's alone. The thanks card says "his answer was made free," passive on purpose; W3C credits him with making it available with no patent or royalties.
7. **Left out:** his 2009 regret about the "//" in URLs (trivia, not a backfire), the 2025 memoir "This Is for Everyone" (not needed), and claims about centralization by a few platforms (his view, but not his decision).
8. The Vanity Fair 2018 "no central authority" quote was verified through a peer-reviewed paper that cites it; the original page could not be fetched.
