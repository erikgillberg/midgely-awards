# Sources: midgley (Thomas Midgley Jr., leaded gasoline and CFCs)

Carousel: `public/carousels/midgley.json`. This file was started on 2026-10-21 for a targeted fact check; it covers the worker deaths and the ledger's gains, not every card.

Key sources, abbreviated below:
- **Kitman 2000**: Jamie Lincoln Kitman, "The Secret History of Lead," *The Nation*, March 2, 2000. https://www.thenation.com/article/archive/secret-history-lead/
- **Kovarik 2005**: William Kovarik, "Ethyl-leaded gasoline: how a classic occupational disease became an international public health disaster," *International Journal of Occupational and Environmental Health* 11(4), 384 to 397 (2005). Author's copy: https://environmentalhistory.org/ijoeh-lead/
- **AOGHS**: B.A. Wells and K.L. Wells, "Ethyl Anti-Knock Gas," American Oil & Gas Historical Society (updated Dec 4, 2025). https://aoghs.org/products/tetraethyl-lead-gasoline
- **Bernstein 2010**: Mark Bernstein, "Freon," *Invention & Technology* 25(3), Fall 2010. https://www.inventionandtech.com/node/86839

| Claim (card) | Source | URL | Supporting line or locator |
|---|---|---|---|
| October 1924: workers making the additive at a New Jersey refinery began to die (twist) | Kitman 2000 | above | "the first of five workers who would die in quick succession at Standard Oil's Bayway TEL works perished" (October 1924) |
| At least 15 workers killed in the plants that made the lead additive (ledger) | Kitman 2000; Kovarik 2005 | above | Kitman: two GM employees at the Dayton pilot plant (April 1924), five at Bayway (October 1924), and "eight more TEL-related deaths and more than 300 injuries at Du Pont's sinister Deepwater plant" (reported late 1925): 2 + 5 + 8 = 15. Kovarik's counts run higher (Bayway "Seven men died," DuPont "ten more," Dayton "at least two"), so "at least 15" is the conservative floor. Neither source states a single total; the sum is ours. |
| Engines that stopped knocking, and more powerful cars and planes (ledger) | AOGHS | above | TEL "yielded gasoline without the loud, power-robbing knock"; advertised "to improve high-compression engine performance"; "The properties of tetraethyl lead proved vital to the Allies during World War II," including for aviation engines. Industry-history source; adequate for a gains line. |
| Fridges that no longer used ammonia or sulfur dioxide, gases that could poison a family (ledger) | Bernstein 2010 | above | "Chemicals used as refrigerants (ammonia, methyl chloride, and sulfur dioxide) were not only toxic but highly combustible." Freon "was nontoxic and nonflammable." (Parentheses replace dashes in the original.) Do not reuse the same article's 1929 Cleveland hospital claim: that disaster is usually attributed to burning X-ray film, not a refrigerant leak. |

## Not sourced

1. **"Cheap cooling for food and medicine" (ledger).** No source found that ties CFCs specifically to cheaper cooling or to medicine. Bernstein reports more than 8 million new refrigerators sold by Freon-using makers within five years of its introduction, which supports wider home refrigeration, not "cheap" or "medicine." Suggested replacement in the fact-check report.
